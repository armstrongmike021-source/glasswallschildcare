Site check

Will your site work for a childcare centre?

Most sites fail on one of ten tests, and the tests are cheap to run before you buy. Here they are, in the order we run them. Send us the address and we run them for you, free, within a few days.

  1. Zoning. Centre-based child care is permitted with consent in R2, R3, R4, the business and mixed-use zones and most others. Rural and conservation zones usually not. We read the zone off the planning portal in a minute.
  2. Size and shape. A 90-place centre wants about 850 m² of building, 630 m² of outdoor area, 25 to 30 car spaces and the setbacks: 1,800 to 2,500 m² on one level, less if the building goes to two storeys or the parking goes under. Frontage of 18 m or more.
  3. Slope. Over 1 in 20 the outdoor area needs terracing and the drop-off gets hard; some councils will not support it.
  4. The road. A main road brings traffic objections, a 55 dB(A) acoustic problem in the yard and, within 125 m of a major road, a safety report. A quiet street off a collector road is the sweet spot.
  5. Hazards. Petrol stations, high-voltage lines (a report within 70 m of a line over 33 kV), mobile base stations (within 500 m), contaminated or former industrial land, flood and bushfire overlays.
  6. Neighbours. A centre next to houses needs the 3 m setbacks, deep-soil planting, an acoustic fence and conditioned hours. Next to a school, a park, a church or shops it is an easier approval.
  7. Trees. Protected trees fix the building footprint before you draw it. Councils want trees kept and more planted.
  8. Parking and drop-off. The parking rate and a drop-off that lets cars leave in a forward direction. On a narrow site the car park eats the yard; on a corner it solves itself.
  9. Orientation. The outdoor area wants the north. A site whose only yard faces south needs a cleverer plan and more shade engineering.
  10. The market. Council cannot refuse a centre for being near another one, but the operator can. We look at the places within two kilometres and the suburbs' ages before we draw.

What a capacity study gives you

A sketch at 1:200 with the building, the outdoor area, the parking and the setbacks blocked out to the rules; the places the site carries by age group; the approval path and the consultants it needs; and the cost from the calculator, refined to the site. Developers use it to decide whether to exchange, operators to decide whether to sign the lease, and the architect starts from a site that has been tested.
